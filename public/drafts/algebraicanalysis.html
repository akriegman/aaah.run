Algebraic Analysis

\(\gdef\Fin{\operatorname{Fin}}\)

$$ \gdef\Fin{\operatorname{Fin}} \gdef\Inf{\operatorname{Inf}} \gdef\N{\mathbb{N}} \gdef\Z{\mathbb{Z}} \gdef\Q{\mathbb{Q}} \gdef\R{\mathbb{R}} $$

Recently I was looking for a calculus textbook to recommend to my little cousins. I asked Chat1, “Is there like a ‘Calculus Done Right’ or ‘Feynman Lectures on Calculus’ type of deal?” The first option it gave me was Spivak, which is great, but the second option was Keisler’s “Elementary Calculus: An Infinitesimal Approach,” which uses Abraham Robinson’s non-standard analysis. Instead of the real numbers the book uses the hyperreal numbers, an extension of the reals that also contains infinitesimal and infinite numbers. This is what I recommended to my cousins.

The hyperreal numbers satisfy the transfer principle, which says that any statement that applies to the real numbers applies to the hyperreals too. When I first read this I thought Keisler was just making stuff up, but after looking into it more I found out that …

There’s something redundant about the hyperreals though. The reals were first constructed from the rationals, back when there was doubt as to whether the reals exist, by taking equivalence classes of sequences of rationals that look like they’re converging to the same point. The hyperreals are constructed from the reals by again taking equivalence classes of sequences of numbers. The relation is different this time though: the sequences of real numbers form a ring, and we take a quotient by a maximal ideal. This is called the ultrapower, and it turns out to be the right way to construct an object satisfying the transfer principle. Both of these constructions give us the continuity we need to make calculus rigorous, but in different ways. Robinson and Keisler straddle both worlds, effectively focusing on bridging this gap.

In my opinion, we should take this alternative approach to continuity seriously. Instead of using the reals or the hyperreals, we should use the hyperrationals. The whole point of the ultrapower is that we take one generalized limit one time, and then we never have to take another limit again. The hyperrationals don’t contain numbers like \(\sqrt{2}\) or \(\pi\), but they do contain numbers infinitely close to these. This means that we’ll have to live with some ambiguity and work mostly with equations that only hold up to an infinitesimal, but as we will see this allows us to replace all the machinery of analysis with some very satisfying ring theory, that looks a lot like modular arithmetic. There’s an argument to be made that this is a more honest approach to irrational numbers, since you can never know \(\sqrt{2}\) in it’s entirety, you can only know successively better rational approximations of it.

Some definitions, notation, and basic properties:

  • \(x \in *\Q\) is infinite if \(|x|\) is greater than all rationals
  • \(x \in *\Q\) is finite if it is not infinite
  • \(\Fin*\Q\), or just \(\Fin\) for short, is the finite hyperrationals. \(\Fin\) is a ring.
  • \(x \in *\Q\) is infinitesimal if \(|x|\) is less than all positive rationals
  • \(\Inf*\Q\), or just \(\Inf\) for short, is the infinitesimal hyperrationals. \(\Inf\) is the unique maximal ideal of \(\Fin\).
  • \(a \approx b\), read “$a$ almost equals $b$”, means \(a-b \in \Inf\)
  • For any term \(S\), “almost $S$” is the term you get by replacing every occurence of \(=\) with \(\approx\). Almost \(\geq\) and almost \(\leq\) will be written \(\gtrsim\) and \(\lesssim\).
  • We will use sets and multivalued expressions in our equations. If \(P(x)\) is a predicate and \(A\) is a set, then by \(P(A)\) we will usually mean \(\exists a\in A, P(a)\) but sometimes \(\forall a\in A, P(a)\), and when we want to be clear we will add a quantifier annotation like \(a = b + E^\exists\). Beware that we will also have ordinary equations between sets2.
  • Recall that a \(\Fin\)-submodule of \(*\Q\), aka a fractional ideal of \(\Fin\), is a subset of \(*\Q\) closed under multiplication by \(\Fin\) and addition with itself. In this case, closure under multiplication by \(\Fin\) implies closure under addition, so we can forget about the second condition. Define a modulus to be a non-zero \(\Fin\)-submodule. Containment is a total ordering on the moduli, and each modulus is convex.
  • \(O(x)\) is the modulus generated by \(x\), ie \(x \cdot \Fin\). This way we can be cute and write things like \(f(x+dx) = f(x) + O(dx)\).
  • \(o(x)\) is the modulus \(x \cdot \Inf\)
  • We will use the linear topology, so the open sets on \(*\Q\) are the cosets of the moduli, and unions thereof.
  • For any function \(f(x)\), we will take \(df\), called the differential of \(f\), to be the function of two variables \(df(x, dx) = f(x+dx)-f(x)\). We will often elide the second argument or both.
  • This subject is usually called “Non-Standard Analysis”, but I won’t be using that term because I think this should be standard. I think “Algebraic Analysis” is probably the right name, because instead of using infinitary methods (Analysis) we’re using finitary methods (Algebra) on infinitary objects.

The quotient \(\Fin/\Inf\) is \(\R\), and the quotient map is the analogue of Robinson’s standard part map. Lots of fun number theoretic and profinite objects arise as various quotients of various subsets of the hyperrationals. For instance if we used a different valuation to define \(\Fin\) and \(\Inf\) then \(\Fin/\Inf\) would be the \(p\)-adics or the \(P\)-adics, a field with characteristic 0 but non-zero hypercharacteristic. The profinite integers are \(*\Z / \{N\in *\Z : n|N \forall n \in \Z\}\).

Completeness

Since our choice of topology feels a little strange, we will justify it by proving that \(f(x)=x^2\) is continuous. We must show that for any modulus coset \(y+E\), the preimage \(f^{-1}(y+E)\) is a union of modulus cosets. Since \(y+E\) is convex, we can break this into three cases:

  • \(0 > y+E\): Squares are positive so \(f^{-1}(y+E)\) is empty.
  • \(0 \in y+E\): Wlog \(y=0\). We will show that \(D=f^{-1}(E)\) is a modulus. Take \(\delta \in E \cap \Fin,\ \neq 0\), then \(\delta^2 \in E\), so \(D\) is non-zero. If \(\lambda \in\Fin\) and \(\delta\in D\), then \(\lambda^2\delta^2 \in E \implies \lambda\delta \in D\).
  • \(0 < y+E\): If \(\exists x \in f^{-1}(y+E)\), wlog take \(x > 0\), and take \(D = \{\delta : f(x+\delta) \in y+E, x + \delta > 0\}\), so that \(f^{-1}(y+E) = -x-D \cup x+D\). (Our next theorem will show that there is such an \(x\), but for this proof it is ok if there isn’t.) We will show that \(D = \frac 1x E\), which is a modulus. If \(\delta \in D\), then \(df = 2x\delta(1 + \frac{\delta}{2x}) \in E\), and \(1 + \frac{\delta}{2x} > \frac12 \implies x\delta \in E\). Conversely take \(\delta \in \frac 1x E\). We must have \(\delta \in o(x)\), otherwise \(-x^2 = -(\frac x \delta)x\delta \in E \implies 0 \in y+E\). So \(df = 2x\delta(1 + o(1)) \in E\) and \(x + \delta = x(1+o(1)) > 0\), \(\implies \delta \in D\). \(\tag*{\square}\)

Note that in this topology there are functions which are continuous but one would not describe as continuous, eg

$$ f(x) = \begin{cases}0 & x < 0 \text{ or } x \in \Inf && 1 & x > 0 \text{ and } x \not\in \Inf\end{cases} $$

However we will show that continuous internal functions behave as we would expect.

Theorem: (Almost Completeness) Let \(A \subset *\Q\) be a nonempty internal set with an upper bound. Then for every modulus \(E\), \(A\) has an almost supremum \(s\), meaning

$$ \begin{align*} A^\forall < s + E^\exists \ b \geq A^\forall \implies b > s + E^\exists \end{align*} $$

In other words, \(*\Q / E\) is “internally complete”3.

Proof: Take \(a\in A\), \(b \geq A^\forall\), and \(N \in *\N\) with \(\frac {b-a}{N} = h \in E\) (eg take \(\epsilon \in E\) and \(N = \lceil \frac{b-a}{\epsilon} \rceil\)

Complex Analysis

Now in order to develop complex analysis for the hyperrationals, we have a few options for what field to work over. We can add:

  • A root of \(x^2+1\)
  • A root of every polynomial
  • A root of every function
  • A root of every \(P\) function for \(P\) = continuous, non-constant, …

The later options are a bit more involved, but the first two are worth taking seriously. Just like how algebraic closure is a theorem for \(\R[i]\), almost algebraic closure is a theorem for \(*Q[i]\), and so it’s cheating to assume it. Subjects that need algebraic closure like representation theory and algebraic geometry can be done with almost algebraic closure, but it may be better to just assume full algebraic closure. For the algebraic closures of the various quotients of \(*\Q\) you’ll probably need the full algebraic closure. For our purposes we’ll use \(C = *\Q[i]\).
